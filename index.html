Paris in November · Base: Ménilmontant

Six days from Ménilmontant

One apartment by Ménilmontant. One main visit each morning. Lunch is the meal you book. Home by late afternoon. The plan follows one theme: how France fought wars, lost power, and started again. Each stop is a place where that happened.

  • 6 days, one apartment
  • Nearest metro: Père Lachaise, lines 2 and 3
  • November: 5 to 10°C, dark by late afternoon
  • Baby: carrier is easier than a stroller

The theme

One story, in the places where it happened

France changed rulers many times. Kings ruled for centuries. The French Revolution of 1789 removed the king and declared a republic. Then Napoleon took power and crowned himself emperor. Then France went back and forth between kings, emperors, and republics. Paris still holds the buildings from each stage.

Three wars matter for this trip. The Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871: Prussia (the state that led the new Germany) invaded France, surrounded Paris for months, and starved the city into surrender. World War I, 1914 to 1918: France and Germany fought again, mostly in trenches in northern France, and France ended on the winning side. World War II, 1939 to 1945: Germany invaded France in 1940 and occupied Paris until August 1944.

Each day maps to that story. The Invalides opens the week: Louis XIV’s hospital for wounded soldiers, Napoleon’s tomb, and the Army Museum through both world wars, including the twentieth-century galleries. Denfert pairs a memorial to the 1870 war with a museum about the Nazi occupation and the Liberation of Paris in 1944. The Louvre is the break from the war story: the palace of French kings, plus Victory, Venus, Leonardo and the Renaissance masters, and Vermeer. Versailles is the stage where the 1871 defeat and the 1919 peace were both performed. Fashion plus the boat on the last full day shows how Paris rebuilt its image after the war, then shows the whole city from the river at sunset.

Your nearest station is Père Lachaise, on lines 2 and 3, a couple of minutes from the apartment. Almost every trip starts on line 3 westward, and the changes happen at three places. At République: line 5 for Gare du Nord and Bastille, line 9 for the 8th district. At Opéra: line 7 for the Louvre and the Cité, line 8 for Concorde and the Invalides. At Saint-Lazare: line 13 for Montparnasse, line 12 for the Orsay. One day goes east instead: line 2 to Nation, then line 6 south to Denfert-Rochereau. Once you take a train from Montparnasse. All map links start from the pin 48.8631 N, 2.3875 E, at the station. Bring a carrier and a stroller. Use the carrier in stations and crowded rooms.

November is grey, about 5 to 10 degrees, and dark by about half past four to half past five.

Day 1 · Monday · Line 3, then Line 8

Les Invalides (Monday)

Official site: Musée de l’Armée

7–12°C · Moderate drizzle1.5 mm rain · Wind 16 km/hNovember sample

Les Invalides is Louis XIV’s hospital for wounded soldiers, with Mansart’s dome over Napoleon’s tomb and the Army Museum in the wings. The Army Museum and the Order of the Liberation museum are open daily, so the first day works. This is the only Invalides day, so include the twentieth-century rooms rather than saving them.

Transit: apartment → Invalides

Open in Google Maps ↗

Take line 3 west to Opéra, then line 8 through Madeleine and Concorde to Invalides, which puts you out under the esplanade. The station has stairs. With a stroller, a taxi to Place Vauban is the best arrival: it drops you at the south door, inside the complex. The dome is the first thing you see.

Louis XIV founded this hospital in 1670 for soldiers wounded in his wars. The architect Libéral Bruant designed the long buildings and the soldiers’ church. Jules Hardouin-Mansart designed the Dome church, finished in 1706. The building makes a claim: the state owes its wounded soldiers a palace, and that palace also shows off state power.

Do three things, in this order, then leave. Three to three and a half hours is a full visit with the baby.

First, the tomb of Napoleon, while everyone is fresh. Napoleon Bonaparte was the general who seized power in 1799, crowned himself emperor in 1804, conquered much of Europe, and was finally exiled to the island of Saint Helena, where he died in 1821. His body was brought back to France in 1840. The tomb was finished in 1861 to a design by Visconti: a red stone coffin at the bottom of a round open well, under the full height of the dome. Give it fifteen quiet minutes. You need no audio guide.

Second, the models of fortress towns (plans-reliefs), usually on an upper floor of the Army Museum. They were started under Louis XIV, when the engineer Vauban built rings of forts around France. Each model shows a whole fortified town from above, with walls the size of your hand. Confirm that floor is open when you book. It is the least famous great thing in the building.

Third, one pass through the Napoleonic galleries, without trying to see every uniform. Then the twentieth-century rooms: one path through 1914 to 1918 (World War I), then 1939 to 1945 (World War II), then the Museum of the Order of the Liberation in the same complex. The Order is an award Charles de Gaulle created in 1940 for heroes of the liberation. Jean Moulin, whom you meet again at Denfert tomorrow, received it. Ninety minutes for this half, then out.

Lunch · 12:30

Café Constant, 139 rue Saint-Dominique: a neighborhood bistro from Christian Constant with classic dishes, fair prices, and no show. Short walk from the dome. Book 12:30 and confirm Monday hours when you book. Route. If it is full, Les Cocottes next door is easier.

Nothing after lunch. If Barthélémy, 51 rue de Grenelle, is open, buy cheese for the apartment. Do not make it a detour.

Instead: the Rodin Museum

The Rodin Museum is closed on Mondays, so it is not this day. If it is the one you want, move this Invalides day to another morning in the week: the Army Museum is open every day and the Rodin is not.

Then see only the dome and the tomb, about an hour, and give the rest of the morning to the Rodin Museum, 77 rue de Varenne, fifteen minutes on foot from the dome: Auguste Rodin (1840 to 1917), the Hôtel Biron mansion as his studio, the Thinker and the Burghers of Calais in the garden. Ninety minutes, then out. Route from the apartment (line 3 to Saint-Lazare, then line 13 to Varenne).

Day 2 · Tuesday · Line 2, then Line 6

Denfert-Rochereau (Tuesday)

Official site: Musée de la Libération

9–15°C · OvercastDry · Wind 19 km/hNovember sample

Denfert-Rochereau is a square in the 14th district where the Lion de Belfort memorial to the 1870 war faces the museum of the Nazi occupation and the 1944 Liberation. The museum is open Tuesday to Sunday, so Tuesday works. Take line 2 two stops east to Nation, then line 6 toward Charles de Gaulle–Étoile: the station comes up in the square. If you want a slow start, walk rue Daguerre, the market street two minutes south of the square. Its shops close on Monday, so Tuesday morning is when it is alive.

Transit: apartment → museum

Open in Google Maps ↗

Two defeats, seventy years apart, share one square.

The Lion de Belfort stands in the middle of the square. It is a copy by the sculptor Bartholdi (who also made the Statue of Liberty) of his lion monument in the city of Belfort. It honors Colonel Denfert-Rochereau, who defended Belfort against the Prussian army in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871. In that war Paris itself was surrounded for months. People starved. France lost and paid a large indemnity. The square and the station are named after Denfert-Rochereau. The lion is the 1870 memorial. It takes five minutes, outdoors.

The museum on the square covers World War II. Germany invaded France in 1940 and occupied Paris for four years. The building is a tollhouse from the 1780s, designed by Ledoux as part of a wall where goods entering Paris were taxed. The museum tells two stories. Jean Moulin was sent by General Charles de Gaulle (who led Free France from London) to unite the French Resistance groups. Moulin chaired their first joint council in 1943, was arrested by the Gestapo (the Nazi secret police), tortured, and died. General Leclerc commanded the French 2nd Armored Division that entered Paris on 24 to 25 August 1944 and freed the city. Book the command-post visit if offered. From a bunker under this square, Colonel Rol-Tanguy directed the Paris fighters during the August 1944 uprising. The bunker is the part visitors miss. It is the part that makes the visit worth it.

Allow about ninety minutes, lion included. Then stop. This day stays small on purpose.

Lunch · 12:30

Le Severo, 8 rue des Plantes, is the butcher’s bistro for this day: it shuts at weekends, so Tuesday is one of the days it opens. The room is small, so book, or eat on Avenue du Général Leclerc, the avenue you arrive on, where the brasseries serve a plain lunch. If you eat out on the avenue, walk back to the square for the museum. Confirm Tuesday hours when you book.

Instead: the Catacombs

One parent visits the Catacombs while the other waits in a café on the square. Do not take the baby down. It is a long stairway, damp tunnels, and no easy exit. Or swap this day with the Louvre if you would rather start with paintings than war.

Day 3 · Wednesday · Line 3, then Line 7

The Louvre (Wednesday)

Official site: Louvre

8–15°C · OvercastDry · Wind 14 km/hNovember sample

The Louvre is the palace of French kings turned museum, from the Greek sculpture downstairs to the Italian and Dutch painting upstairs. Enter through the Carrousel shopping level, not the pyramid. That entrance is step-free and connects to the station.

Transit: apartment → Louvre

Open in Google Maps ↗

Take line 3 west to Opéra, then line 7 two stops to Palais-Royal–Musée du Louvre, which opens into the Carrousel level. Line 1 is the second way in, from Châtelet, if you would rather walk the pyramid side. Wednesday is the Louvre’s late night, so the morning is quieter than the afternoon.

Do not tour the Louvre. Use it. Two and a half hours from opening, then out. This day is the break from the war story: sculpture and painting, not battle painting.

The building is still the frame. A fortress stood here around 1190 under King Philippe Auguste. King Charles V made it a residence. King François I started the palace. Every later ruler extended it. The glass pyramid from 1989 is only the newest front door. If you want the medieval moat in the Sully basement, give it ten minutes at the start, then go up to the art. Do not let it eat the morning.

Walk this order. It keeps the stairs and backtracking down with a baby in a carrier.

  • Winged Victory of Samothrace, top of the Daru staircase in Denon. Go first, while everyone is fresh and before the groups arrive. Hellenistic, 2nd century BC, found on Samothrace in 1863. Headless, standing into wind on a ship prow. Ten minutes is enough.
  • Venus de Milo, Sully ground floor. Short walk from the staircase. Greek, around 100 BC, found on Milos in 1820. Missing arms, still the standard for the female figure. Same deal: look, then move.
  • Italian Renaissance, Denon first floor. This is the core. Leonardo da Vinci: the Mona Lisa plus the Virgin of the Rocks, the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne, Saint John the Baptist, and La Belle Ferronnière, all in the same wing. See the Mona Lisa because you are there, then spend the real time on the other Leonardos and the large Veronese Wedding at Cana hanging opposite it. Raphael’s La Belle Jardinière and the Saint Michael paintings, Titian, Tintoretto, and Caravaggio’s Death of the Virgin are in the same rooms. Forty-five minutes for this run.
  • Dutch masters, Richelieu second floor. Vermeer’s The Lacemaker and The Astronomer hang here, the Louvre’s two Vermeers. Small, quiet, easy to miss after the big Italian rooms. Rembrandt’s Bathsheba and the Supper at Emmaus are nearby if the baby still allows it. Thirty minutes, then out.

The Mona Lisa room will be crowded. Do not fight it. Let the crowd have the wall, check the painting, then turn around: the Veronese and the Leonardos around you are the better viewing.

David’s Coronation, Gros’s Jaffa, Géricault’s Raft, Delacroix’s Liberty all hang together in Daru and Mollien if you want them, but they are the week’s old argument. This morning is for Victory, Venus, Leonardo, Raphael, Veronese, and Vermeer.

Lunch · 12:30

Willi’s Wine Bar, 13 rue des Petits-Champs, open since 1980. The wine list is the reason. The food is simple bistro cooking, which suits a baby, and the room is calm. Book 12:30. Route from the Louvre.

Restaurant du Palais Royal, 110 Galerie de Valois, is the alternative if you want to eat inside historic arcades rather than in a wine bar. In November you sit indoors, which helps after Denon. Route. If Willi’s is full, Juveniles, 47 rue de Richelieu, is the smaller wine bar nearby.

Instead: the Orangerie Museum

The Orangerie museum stands at the Concorde end of the Tuileries garden, a short walk from the exit. It is closed Tuesdays. Monet’s Water Lilies fill two oval rooms, installed after his death as a full environment, not a row of pictures. He gave them to France as a memorial to the peace after World War I. They answer the Army Museum. Forty-five minutes. If you are done, you are done. Route from the apartment (line 3 to Opéra, line 8 to Concorde). Photographs.

Instead: the Orsay Museum

Choose the Orsay museum if you wake up wanting nineteenth-century art more than the palace. Take line 3 to Saint-Lazare, then line 12 to Solférino: the museum is two minutes from the exit. It is closed Mondays. Never try Orsay and the Louvre on the same day.

Day 4 · Thursday · Line 3, then Line 13 to Montparnasse

Versailles (Thursday)

Official site: Palace of Versailles

12–17°C · OvercastDry · Wind 17 km/hNovember sample

Versailles is Louis XIV’s palace, and the Hall of Mirrors is the one room to go for: the stage of the 1871 German Empire proclamation and the 1919 peace treaty.

Transit: apartment → palace

Open in Google Maps ↗

Take line 3 west to Saint-Lazare, change to line 13 toward Châtitillon–Montrouge, and ride to Montparnasse–Bienvenüe. From there the Transilien train to Versailles-Chantiers takes about fifteen minutes, then a taxi or a fifteen-minute walk up to the palace. Door to door is about an hour and a quarter if connections work. The other line, RER C from Saint-Michel to Versailles-Château-Rive-Gauche, stops closer to the gate, but getting to Saint-Michel with a stroller means another change. Use Chantiers unless you want to arrive at the door. Chantiers · Rive-Gauche

The Hall of Mirrors was built between 1678 and 1684 by Hardouin-Mansart and Le Brun. It was the ceremonial gallery of the absolute monarchy: seventeen windows on one side, seventeen mirrored arches on the other, the park outside and the court inside. On 18 January 1871, while Paris was still surrounded in the Franco-Prussian War, the German Empire was proclaimed in this room. The place was chosen to humiliate France. On 28 June 1919 the treaty that ended World War I was signed in the same room. That was the symbolic reversal. The Army Museum showed the wars. This is the stage where the defeat and the peace were both performed. Stand there long enough to see it as a stage, not as gold decoration.

The rest of the palace is the machine those later wars answered. Louis XIV moved the court here in 1682 and forced the nobility to live around him on a schedule he controlled. Walk the royal apartments with that in mind. Two hours inside is enough.

From the terrace, look once at Le Nôtre’s garden axes. The same idea runs through the whole French garden tradition, and you will not see a bigger version. In November the fountains are off and the gardens are not the visit. Do not add the Trianon palaces.

Lunch

Ore, in the Pavillon Dufour, is Alain Ducasse’s restaurant inside the palace. Book it. It avoids another transfer with a tired baby, and the cooking is a real meal, not a museum café. It is the nicest room of the week’s lunches. A carrier is easier than a stroller.

To leave the palace, taxi five minutes to the Saint-Louis district and book a simple bistro there. Eat, then take the train. Plan nothing after you get back to Ménilmontant.

Instead: the Château de Vincennes

This is not a lesser day. It is the sterner building, and it will be empty.

Transit: apartment → Vincennes

Open in Google Maps ↗

Take line 2 two stops to Nation, then line 1 east to the last stop. The château is at the station. This commute is easier than Versailles.

The keep was raised in the 1300s. At about fifty meters it is the tallest medieval keep in Europe. You can still read it as a weapon. The chapel was started under King Charles V and finished under King François I. It has fine stained glass and no queue. The moat was used for executions more than once. On 21 March 1804 the Duke of Enghien was tried by a rigged court and shot here on Napoleon’s order. The political murder shocked Europe. Under the Nazi occupation the fortress was a German base, and resistance fighters were shot in the moat. A memorial marks the spot. Versailles is the diplomatic stage. Vincennes is the fortress itself.

The town starts at the gate. Book a simple table there instead of returning to Paris hungry. Choose Versailles or Vincennes. Do not do both.

Normandy looks more ambitious and is not. It means most of a day in transit for one rushed beach, and it wastes the apartment. Not from Ménilmontant, not with this baby.

Day 5 · Friday · Line 3, then Line 9

Galerie Dior, then the boat (Friday)

Official sites: Galerie Dior · Bateaux Parisiens

14–19°C · Dense drizzle2.1 mm rain · Wind 13 km/hNovember sample

Galerie Dior at 11 rue François Ier is the fashion exhibition that closes the week, showing how Paris rebuilt its image after the war. Take line 3 to République, then line 9 west to Franklin D. Roosevelt: the gallery is a five-minute walk from the Champs-Élysées exit. The gallery is open Friday (it closes Tuesdays).

Transit: apartment → gallery

Open in Google Maps ↗

The gallery sits at 11 rue François Ier, beside 30 Avenue Montaigne, the house where Christian Dior showed his first collection. On 12 February 1947 the New Look brought back the narrow waist and the full skirt after years of wartime fabric rationing. It was praised, and it was attacked as wasteful in a country still short of fabric. That argument links it to the war theme of the week. The gallery is a designed exhibition, more spectacular than quiet. Only tickets bought on the gallery site guarantee entry, so book there. Allow ninety minutes. Galerie Dior.

Lunch, lighter than usual

You have a boat at 4. Taxi down to the 7th district, which is familiar ground by now. Fontaine de Mars, 129 rue Saint-Dominique, is the table for this day, since Café Constant was Monday’s; if that lunch fell through, take Café Constant instead. Keep it to an hour and keep a rest before the pier. To walk to a table instead, Le Relais Plaza at the Plaza Athénée, 25 Avenue Montaigne, is the fashion-history room: expensive, formal, and only worth it if a grand hotel dining room sounds like the point rather than a risk with a baby.

Then the river. The Alma pier is in this neighborhood, so keep the rest between lunch and the boat. If you have the legs, walk toward Pont Alexandre III. If not, taxi to the pier.

The bridge was built for the 1900 world fair and named after the Russian tsar. It marked the alliance between France and Russia. It is a single low steel arch, so it does not block the view, and it sits on the axis running from the Invalides dome. Gold statues, lamp posts, the Grand Palais beyond.

The boat · sailing nearest 4pm

One hour, sightseeing only, not dinner and not a show. Book Bateaux Parisiens or Vedettes de Paris from Port de la Bourdonnais, at Pont de l’Alma, on the sailing closest to 4pm. Choose a larger boat. Sit outside for the twenty minutes that matter if it is dry, inside if the wind is up. Pier from the apartment. Bateaux Parisiens.

The Eiffel Tower will appear from the water as the lights replace the sky. That is the version worth having. The river in photographs.

Home by about 6. Take one cruise total, never two.

Instead: the Mémorial de la Shoah

Choose this only if you want the other half of the occupation story. Then the day ends there. Do not combine it with boutiques.

Transit from the apartment

The memorial stands on rue Geoffroy-l’Asnier, in the Marais. Take line 3 to République, then line 5 one stop to Bastille, and walk ten minutes through the Marais. The Liberation museum told the military story of the occupation. This tells who was taken from the city, with help from the French state. The Vichy regime was the French government that cooperated with Germany. French police carried out the roundup of July 1942 and the deportations to Nazi death camps. The Wall of Names lists about 76,000 Jews deported from France. The wall and the reading room are the visit. It is indoors and works with a carrier. It is closed on Saturdays.

Allow seventy-five to ninety minutes. Then a quiet lunch, not a wine crawl. Breizh Café, 109 rue Vieille du Temple: buckwheat pancakes and cider, calm, easy with a baby. Route from the memorial. Then home. If that weight does not fit this trip, the fashion house is a fine choice. The memorial.

Instead: the Conciergerie

This is war inside the city, a century and a half before the world wars.

Transit from the apartment

Take line 3 to Opéra, then line 7 toward Villejuif or Mairie d’Ivry and get off at Pont Neuf: the Conciergerie is four minutes away along the river. Avoid Cité station, which is all stairs and awkward with a stroller. Wear the baby, or taxi there and take the metro home.

The Conciergerie was the medieval royal palace on the island in the Seine, then a prison. The Hall of Soldiers was built in the early 1300s under King Philippe le Bel. It is one of the finest Gothic halls in Europe. During the French Revolution this building was the waiting room for the guillotine. Marie-Antoinette, the queen, was held here before her execution on 16 October 1793. The Revolutionary court sat next door and sent thousands to their deaths in 1793 to 1794. See the hall and the cell. Skip the wax figures. An hour to seventy-five minutes.

Sainte-Chapelle is two hundred meters away. King Louis IX built it in the 1240s to hold the Crown of Thorns, a relic believed to come from Jesus. The upper chapel is walls of stained glass. Twenty minutes there can be worth it even in weak November light. Go in only if you are already at the door and you want the glass. It is not the reason for the day. Book if you go. Photographs.

Lunch: Le Reminet, 3 rue des Grands Degrés, a small serious bistro across the bridge in the 5th district. Book. Route. Brasserie de l’Isle Saint-Louis, 55 quai de Bourbon, is the easier table, with a view and more tourists.

Vedettes du Pont Neuf leaves from the tip of the island. This is the other good boat, at the same 4pm hour: medieval Paris from the water, not the grand axis of the 7th district. Only if you did not sail from Alma.

Day 6 · Saturday

Out (Saturday)

10–14°C · Moderate rain24.4 mm rain · Wind 12 km/hNovember sample

The last morning stays local. Sleep in, coffee, no new museum, no new line. If the flight is late, repeat whichever lunch you liked best and come straight back.

Both airports are straightforward by transit. For Charles de Gaulle, take line 3 to République, line 5 to Gare du Nord, then the RER B. There are lifts at Gare du Nord but the platform change is long, so leave an extra half hour. For Orly, take line 3 to Saint-Lazare, then line 14 straight to the airport; both legs are step-free, which matters with the stroller and the bags. A taxi to either airport is still the calmer choice, and it is the one that keeps the morning unhurried.
